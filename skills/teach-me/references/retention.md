# Making it stick

How to make learning last past the end of the conversation. Read it when closing a session, planning review across days, making flashcards or a quiz, or when a learner says it "won't stick."

## 1. What the evidence supports

Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rated ten popular study techniques on how well they work across learners, materials and delays:

| Technique | Rating | Use it as |
|---|---|---|
| Practice testing (retrieval) | High | The backbone: ask the learner to bring it back out |
| Distributed practice (spacing) | High | Return plans; bringing earlier ideas back later |
| Elaborative interrogation ("why is this true?") | Moderate | One "why" question after a fact or step |
| Self-explanation | Moderate | "Why does step 3 come before step 4?" |
| Interleaved practice | Moderate | Mixed practice on confusable ideas |
| Summarization | Low | Only for learners already good at it |
| Highlighting | Low | Don't recommend it |
| Keyword mnemonics | Low | Narrow use; doesn't last |
| Imagery for text | Low | Narrow use |
| Rereading | Low | Don't build a plan on it |

The authors caution that none of these is a cure-all, and the ratings rest heavily on college students and memory tests. Low means "less effective than the alternatives," not "never." But the pattern is clear: what feels productive (rereading, highlighting, a tidy summary from the tutor) helps less than what feels harder (pulling it back out of memory, spread over time).

## 2. Desirable difficulty, and telling the learner

Conditions that make practice feel easy often produce weaker retention, and conditions that feel harder often produce stronger retention (Bjork's "desirable difficulties": spacing, interleaving, testing). The learner's sense of progress is a poor guide. A difficulty is only desirable if they can succeed at it with effort; one they can't succeed at is just a wall.

Two consequences:

- **Tell them.** A learner who expects smooth review will read struggle as failure. Say it once: "This will feel harder than rereading your notes. That's the point; pulling it out of memory is what makes it stay."
- **Calibrate.** Use the observed state (errors, hints needed) to keep the difficulty at "succeeds with effort." If they're missing most items, drop back; if they're acing everything, space it further or mix in harder items.

One boundary: if the test is very soon after studying, massed practice can beat spacing. Spacing pays off over days.

## 3. Within one session: the sequence

You can't run true spacing in one conversation, but you can use retrieval, self-explanation, generation and mixing.

1. **Retrieve before re-teaching.** Before explaining a related idea, ask for the last one. "Before we add the next piece: what did we say causes the merge conflict?" A wrong or empty answer is information, and it's cheaper to fix now.
2. **Generate before showing.** A prediction or first attempt before the demonstration (section 4).
3. **Self-explain the worked example.** "Why does step 3 come before step 4? What would break if we skipped it?"
4. **Interleave confusable items.** After teaching two similar ideas, mix them (section 5).
5. **Re-ask later, on a new surface.** Bring back an earlier idea after other material, with the answer hidden until they commit. Tell them it's coming: "I'll check this again in a bit."
6. **Close with learner-produced recall** (section 6).

How much a gap of minutes helps, compared with days, isn't well measured. Treat in-session re-asking as a weaker cousin of spacing, still worth doing because it's also a check for the illusion of understanding.

## 4. Generation: make them produce it first

Ask for the learner's version before giving yours. It works as diagnosis (you see their model), as engagement (constructive beats passive in the ICAP framework), and as a hook (your explanation answers their own question).

- "Before I show the output, what do you expect?"
- "What do you think the next step is?"
- "Guess what this word means from the sentence."
- "Sketch the graph you'd expect, then I'll show the real one."
- "Try to define it in one sentence before I do."

The guess doesn't need to be right. A wrong guess still shows you what to fix, and it gives your explanation something to correct. (Studies often report that a wrong guess followed by feedback is remembered better than the answer alone; that wasn't verified for this skill, so treat it as a likely bonus, not the reason.) Give generation prompts only when the learner has something to generate from: asking a true novice to predict with no basis produces confabulation, not learning.

## 5. Interleaving: when to mix, when not to

A meta-analysis (Brunmair and Richter 2019; 59 studies) found interleaving helps on average (g = 0.42), with clear boundaries:

- **Helps most** when the categories are easy to confuse with each other: telling painting styles apart (g = 0.67), choosing the right method for math problems (g = 0.34).
- **No benefit** for expository texts.
- **Hurts** for learning word lists (blocking won, g = −0.39). So for new vocabulary, learn a set together first, then mix later.

The reason interleaving works on confusable problem types: in blocked practice, the learner knows which method to use because it's the one in the chapter title. Mixed practice makes them choose, and choosing is the skill.

**How to apply it:**

- After teaching two similar ideas, give a mixed set where they must decide which applies.
- Include at least one item where either could work, and ask them to argue which they'd pick.

Example, git:

> Five situations. For each, say whether you'd merge or rebase, and why.
> 1. Your local feature branch, not pushed yet, is behind main.
> 2. A shared branch three people are committing to.
> 3. You want to keep a record of when the feature joined main.
> 4. You want a clean, linear history before opening a pull request.
> 5. You've already pushed and a teammate has pulled your branch.

Example, statistics:

> For each question, which do you need: mean, median or mode?
> 1. Typical house price in a city with a few mansions.
> 2. Most common shoe size to stock.
> 3. Average test score in a class with no outliers.

## 6. Closing routines

End with the learner's recall, not your summary. A summary you write feels useful and does less than one sentence they write themselves.

**Short session (one concept):**

> Before we stop: in one or two sentences, without scrolling up, why does a LEFT JOIN keep Ben's row?

**Procedure:**

> Last one, no help from me: a `products` table and a `reviews` table. List every product with its number of reviews, including products with none.

**Longer session:**

> Let's close it out. Three things:
> 1. List the main ideas we covered, from memory.
> 2. Which one feels shakiest?
> 3. One fresh problem: …

Then:

- **Check and fill in.** Correct anything wrong, add anything missing, briefly.
- **Name what to practice next**, specifically. "The thing to practice is resolving a conflict mid-rebase. Try it on a throwaway repo this week."
- **Offer the return plan** (section 7).

If the session ends abruptly ("thanks, gotta go"), a one-line version still helps: "Before you go: what's the one thing you'd tell a friend about this?"

## 7. Across sessions: the return plan

Spaced retrieval over days is the high-utility version of distributed practice. You can't start a conversation, and a page can't send reminders, so the plan has to live in the learner's hands.

**Give them questions to keep, and dates.**

> Before you close this: here are four questions, with no answers here. Put them in your notes. Try them on Thursday without looking, then again next week. If you miss two or more, paste them back to me and we'll re-teach just those.
>
> 1. …
> 2. …

**Suggested intervals.** Expanding gaps are standard practice: roughly one to two days, then about a week, then two to four weeks. A miss means the next gap is shorter, not the same. (The specific schedule is practitioner convention; the evidence that spacing works is strong, the evidence for any exact schedule wasn't checked.) Give actual dates when you know today's date: "Thursday the 13th, then Wednesday the 19th."

**If your environment can create calendar events or reminders** and the learner wants it, offer to add them. Don't do it unasked.

**If you have memory across conversations,** note what the learner found shaky and what they mastered, so the next session can open with retrieval on the shaky items. Tell the learner you've noted it.

**In an artifact,** build a "come back later" section: a short mixed set from all sections, labeled with suggested days, answers revealed only after a typed or selected commit. Say plainly that nothing will remind them. If the page saves state locally, note that it's tied to this browser and can be cleared.

## 8. When a learner comes back

Start with retrieval, not a recap.

> Welcome back. Before I remind you of anything: what do you remember about how rebase handles conflicts?

Then:

- Re-teach only what's missing.
- If they've forgotten most of it, that's normal, and relearning usually goes faster than the first time (practitioner experience). Run a short version of the original sequence.
- If they remember it well, skip ahead and lengthen the next gap.
- If you don't have memory and they don't remember what you covered, ask them to paste their notes or the questions you gave them.

## 9. Review that records misses

A review schedule that only moves forward is a calendar, not a memory model. The failed guitar course had a "Reviewed, schedule next" button with no way to record a miss: the gap widened on skills the learner had already lost.

Every review, in chat or in an artifact:

- Is a short probe they can pass or fail (name ten random notes on the D string; write the query; conjugate five verbs), not "did you review it?"
- Records the result.
- On a miss, shortens the next interval (back to a day or two) and, if it's missed twice, brings back teaching, not only testing.
- On a pass, lengthens it.

## 10. Flashcards and quizzes, when asked

When the learner asks for flashcards or a quiz, make them. Design for retrieval:

- **One idea per card.** "What does `git rebase` do to the original commits?" beats "Explain git rebase."
- **Ask for production, not recognition,** where possible. Typed answers over multiple choice for things they'll need to recall cold.
- **Vary the angle.** For an important idea, two or three cards from different directions (definition, example, "which of these is a case of it", "why does it…").
- **Include "why" cards,** not only "what" cards.
- **For multiple choice, make each wrong option a real misconception,** and explain each one.
- **Mix topics** in a quiz, especially confusable ones.
- **Show the shape of the material,** not a flat term list: group cards under the idea they belong to.
- **Verify every answer.** Compute or run anything that can be computed.

In the Quantum Country experiments (Matuschak and Nielsen), questions embedded in an essay at expanding intervals produced lasting recall for most readers, and atomic, varied questions worked better than broad ones. Their evidence is preliminary and self-selected, and they note card recall doesn't guarantee the learner can use the idea.

## 11. Study advice to give, and to stop giving

**Give:**

- "Test yourself instead of rereading. Close the notes and write what you remember, then check."
- "Spread it out: three 20-minute sessions on different days beat one hour tonight."
- "Mix problem types, so you practice choosing the method."
- "When you get one wrong, figure out why before moving on."
- "Explain it to someone, or out loud to yourself, without looking."

**Stop giving (as a main strategy):**

- "Reread your notes." "Highlight the key points." Both rated low utility.
- "Make a summary." Only works for people already good at summarizing.
- Anything based on learning styles ("since you're a visual learner…"). Matching format to a style doesn't improve learning.
- Retention percentages ("you remember 10% of what you read"). They have no research behind them.

**For an exam tomorrow:** spacing isn't available, so lean on retrieval: practice tests under exam conditions, check answers, re-study only the misses, sleep. Say honestly that cramming works for tomorrow and fades fast, and offer a return plan if they need it to last.

## 12. Transfer

Remembering it isn't the same as being able to use it somewhere new. Transfer needs varied practice with the same structure and the learner explaining why.

- After a worked example, change the surface (names, numbers, context) and keep the structure.
- Then change the structure slightly and ask what still holds: "This worked for a sorted list. Does it still work if the list isn't sorted?"
- Ask "what's the same, and what's different?" between two problems.
- End units with a task that looks different from the examples.

Near transfer (similar problems) is much easier than far transfer (a different context). Don't assume the second follows from the first; test it.

## 13. Evidence notes

- Technique ratings: Strong (Dunlosky et al. 2013, opened).
- Desirable difficulties, performance vs learning: Moderate (Bjork via APS Observer).
- Interleaving boundaries: Moderate (Brunmair and Richter 2019 abstract, opened; Dunlosky rating Moderate).
- In-session spacing benefit: not measured in the sources read; design choice.
- Expanding-interval schedule: Practitioner. Quantum Country: Practitioner/preliminary (Matuschak and Nielsen, opened).
- Generation before showing: supported via ICAP (Moderate) and the prediction findings in notes C, E and I; the generation effect itself was not verified.
- Myths (learning styles, pyramid percentages): Strong evidence against (Pashler et al. 2008; the cone-of-learning history).
- Transfer guidance: Practitioner, supported by the self-explanation rating.
