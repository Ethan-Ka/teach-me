# Teaching motor skills and music

Instruments, sport, drawing, dance, typing, handwriting, anything done with the body. You can't see or hear the learner, and neither can an artifact you build (no microphone pitch detection, no computer vision). So self-assessment is part of the deal, and most of the teaching is practice design. Much of this file is Practitioner; there's still no study of coaching a physical skill you can't observe. Motor-learning research (note J) now backs a few parts, at abstract level and mostly from lab tasks: self-video with something to look for, practice strategy over hours, and, with real disputes, result-focused cues and mixed practice. The heaviest input is a guitar teacher's teardown of a Claude-built course that failed.

## 1. Where the general rules change

- **Telling is a small part.** Describing the correct movement (where the fingers go, how the weight shifts) helps, but most of the value is in what the learner practices, how, and how they check it.
- **The loop is "do this, tell me what happened."** Not "answer this question."
- **You're blind and deaf to the performance.** Say so. Work from the learner's reports, and design the checks so their reports carry information.
- **The learner's own judgment is the skill being trained,** and it's unreliable early. Give them outside references to check against.
- **Bodies get hurt.** A teacher in the room watches for tension and strain. You can't, so you name the risks and build in self-checks.

## 2. Start from what they already do

Ask first: "What can you play right now? Which chords, songs, scales? What do you want to be able to play?" Then build the first steps on that.

The failed guitar course was written for an imagined player and made a real one wait about three months before getting back to the pentatonic scale they already knew. The teacher's version of week one used the learner's existing box shape on day one, with the new knowledge (note names on two strings) attached to it.

- A player who knows five open chords: their next steps use those chords (changes between them, a strumming pattern, a song with them), not a theory unit.
- A runner who wants to get faster: start from their current pace and weekly distance, not a generic plan.
- A sketcher: start from what they draw now.

Prerequisites arrive when a task needs them, attached to that task.

## 3. Songs (and real use) early

Use the new skill in real music within the first session or two. A guitar teacher's estimate of where the failed course's learner would quit: weeks two to five, after three weeks of drills with no song. Every phase should name a piece of real music that uses only what's been learned, with a concrete target ("by the end of week 2: the verse of this song, at 70 bpm, recorded once").

The same holds elsewhere: a real rally in tennis, a real sentence in a language, a real sketch of something they care about.

## 4. The smallest unit that fails

When something doesn't work, shrink the practice to the smallest piece that still contains the problem.

- Not "practice the song." "Practice the change from bar 4 into bar 5, only those two chords."
- Not "work on your serve." "Toss only, ten times. Does the ball land in front of your lead foot?"
- Not "draw better hands." "Draw only the thumb joint, five times, from the reference photo."

Then build back up: the unit, then the unit with one beat before and after, then the phrase, then the section.

**Stop and reset after an error.** Repeating a mistake rehearses the mistake. "If it goes wrong, stop, slow down, and start the unit again. Don't play through it."

## 5. Tempo ladders and slow practice

Expert musicians commonly practice hard passages slowly and in small sections (survey and interview evidence; no controlled trial found). Some slow practice doesn't transfer to full speed, so the ladder has to climb.

**A tempo ladder:**

> Find the tempo where you can play the change cleanly 4 times out of 5. Start there.
> 50 bpm ☐ 55 ☐ 60 ☐ 65 ☐ 70 ☐ 75 ☐ 80 ☐
> Tick a box only after 5 clean in a row. Move up one step at a time. If you miss twice at a new tempo, drop back one step.

Small increments (about 4 to 5 bpm) are common practice. Use a metronome (any free metronome app or website works; an artifact can include a simple one).

The same structure works for typing (words per minute at a target accuracy), running (pace), juggling (catches in a row), and drawing (time per sketch at a set accuracy).

## 6. Observable criteria

A criterion a stranger watching could verify. Not a feeling.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Get comfortable with the C chord" | "Place the C chord from scratch in under 3 seconds, every string ringing, 5 times in a row" |
| "Play it smoothly" | "Every change lands on beat 1 with the click at 60 bpm, 8 bars without stopping" |
| "Sounds good" | "Recorded take: no buzzing strings, no gaps at the changes, steady with the click" |
| "Improve your backhand" | "10 backhands cross-court, count how many land past the service line" |
| "Know the notes on the neck" | "Name 20 random frets on the low E and A strings in under 40 seconds, with a tuner confirming" |
| "Draw in perspective" | "In your cube, the receding edges meet at one point when you extend them with a ruler" |

Each criterion should be runnable alone, in one session, with what the learner has (a metronome, a phone, a tuner, a ruler, a timer). "Someone names the chord" needs a partner; "sounds arranged" can't be checked; "for three months" isn't a session. Rewrite those or supply the tool.

## 7. Record and compare

Listening back catches what playing hides; watching back catches what doing hides. Most learners have a phone.

> Record 30 seconds. Play it back once without stopping. Then answer:
> 1. Did every note ring clearly? (yes / no, which ones didn't)
> 2. Did you stay with the click? (yes / drifted ahead / drifted behind / lost it at bar __)
> 3. Did you stop or hesitate anywhere? (where?)

- **Compare to a reference.** A recording of the target (from a lesson video, a recording of the song) next to theirs.
- **Compare to yourself.** Keep the day-1 recording. Record the same passage on day 7 and compare against the same checklist. Visible progress is a strong reason to keep going.
- **Slow-motion video** for technique: the picking hand, a golf swing, a tennis toss. Give a short checklist of what to look for.

Video of one's own performance helped motor learning and beat verbal feedback alone in physical-education studies, and learners who chose when to watch learned more (Moderate: Mödinger, Woll and Wagner 2021, a systematic review of 11 school studies; Ste-Marie, Vertes, Law and Rymal 2013, one study of children on a trampoline). A novice watching a take may not know what to look at, which is why the three-item checklist matters (Practitioner).

## 8. The "not yet" path

Every criterion gets a named next step for failure, and "not yet" is presented as the normal result of a first try.

| Report | Next step |
|---|---|
| "The C chord buzzes on the B string" | "Your index finger is probably flat or mid-fret. Press just behind the fret wire with the fingertip. Place and lift that one finger 10 times, checking the B string each time." |
| "I can't make the change in time" | "Drop to 40 bpm. Practice only the move: last beat of G into the first beat of C. Lead with the finger that moves least." |
| "My hand hurts" | Stop. See section 12. |
| "I keep rushing at the changes" | "Count out loud. Record it. Is the rush on the beat before the change? Practice that beat alone, with the click." |
| "Not yet" twice on the same rung | Change the plan: a smaller unit, a prerequisite drill, or a different fingering. Don't repeat the same drill a third time. |

In an artifact: a "Not yet" button that opens the smaller step or a pre-filled "tell Claude what happened" message. It changes what comes next.

## 9. The report-back loop

Give the learner a template, so their report carries information:

> When you've practiced, tell me:
> - What you practiced, at what tempo
> - How many clean out of how many tries
> - Where it breaks (which bar, which finger, which string)
> - Anything that felt tense or hurt

Example report: "G to C at 60: 3 of 5 clean. Misses both times were the ring finger landing late. Fine at 50. Wrist feels a bit tight after 10 minutes."

Then diagnose from the pattern (fails only at the change, only at speed, only after a few minutes) and give one adjustment.

Say what you can't judge: "I can't hear the tone, so I'm going on your description. If something sounds off and you can't say what, record it and describe the exact moment."

If the learner can share a recording, photo or video and your environment can take it, use it, and say what you can and can't judge from it. Don't make confident technique claims you can't check.

## 10. Cues, feedback timing and practice order

- **Ask for their judgment before giving yours.** "What did you notice?" first, then your read, and don't comment on every attempt. The idea that less frequent feedback builds independence is Contested in the lab, but in chat reports arrive in batches anyway, and their own judgment is the skill being trained.
- **Ask for counts, not feelings.** "Out of 10 tries at 60 bpm, how many were clean? Which beat drifts?" beats "how did it go?" Use the count to set the next step (4 of 5 clean: up one notch; under 3 of 5: slow down), and say plainly that it's self-reported. Novices tend to overrate themselves, so prefer checks with an outside reference: the metronome, a recording, a tuner (Practitioner).
- **Give a countable exit criterion for each stage.** Popular guitar courses stage practice around one: time a minute of changes between two chords, count them, and move on past a stated number. A number a learner can reach and report beats "until it feels smooth" (Practitioner; the exact thresholds are creators' targets).
- **Prefer cues about the result over cues about the body,** as a default to test. "Let the string ring clean" over "curl your finger more"; "aim the chisel edge at the line" over "keep your elbow in". A large meta-analysis favored this external focus (Chua et al. 2021), but a 2024 Bayesian reanalysis that corrects for publication bias puts the effects near zero (g from 0.01 to 0.15; McKay et al. 2024), and the studies are lab tasks with simple targets. Contested. Use a body cue when the learner can't make the movement at all.
- **Blocked first, mixed later.** Repeat the one change until it's clean, then in a later session mix variants (A to D, D to E, E to A in random order). Warn them mixed practice feels harder and looks worse on the day. Contested: the benefit of mixing shows mostly in lab studies and older adults, and is small and non-significant in applied studies.
- **Plan practice by strategy, not minutes.** In one study of pianists, what they did in practice (targeting errors, slowing down) predicted next-day performance better than how long they practiced (Moderate, small: Duke, Simmons and Cash 2009, one session).
- **Run it as a submit-and-review loop.** "Record one minute. Check these three things. Come back and tell me. Here's your one assignment until then." This is how online music coaches work asynchronously (Practitioner, vendor sources only).

## 11. Perception before symbol (ear and feel)

For a perceptual-motor skill, the named fact has to be tied to the sensation it stands for, in the drill itself. The failed guitar course described sounds in adjectives ("bright," "a hair sharp") and taught note names as a purely visual task, and its listening checks depended on an ear it hadn't trained yet.

What to do (v0, practice-based; ear-training research wasn't covered):

- **Hear the target first.** Point to a recording of the exact sound, or if an artifact can play a tone or chord (in-page audio synthesis is a page-level feature, not an app build), let it play the target next to the notation.
- **Sing, tap or say it before playing.** "Sing the root, then find it." "Clap the rhythm, then play it." This makes the learner form the target in their head.
- **Ask what they hear before you say anything.** "Does the buzz stop when you press closer to the fret?"
- **Use an outside reference for checks the ear can't make yet:** a tuner for pitch, a metronome for time, a recording for comparison.
- **Train the ear alongside the hands** from the start, in short doses, rather than as a later unit.

## 12. Physical safety

Name the risks a teacher in the room would catch, and add a self-check for each. Never make a risky movement a pass requirement.

- **Pain means stop.** Say it plainly: "Sharp pain, numbness or tingling: stop for the day. Mild fingertip soreness in the first weeks of guitar is normal; pain in the wrist, forearm or thumb joint isn't."
- **Tension.** "Every few minutes, check: shoulders up? jaw tight? thumb squeezing? Shake out and reset."
- **Short sessions early.** Several short sessions beat one long one for hands and joints that aren't conditioned.
- **Warm up** before stretches, bends or fast passages.
- **Risky moves need extra care:** wide stretches low on the neck, string bends with weaker fingers, thumb-over grips, overhead serves, heavy lifts, repetitive typing with bent wrists. Introduce them gradually, with a filmed self-check against a short posture checklist.
- **Don't require them.** The failed course made whole-step bends with each of the four fingers (including the unsupported pinky) a pass requirement. Drop risky movements from pass criteria.
- **For sport and exercise:** if the learner mentions an injury, pain or a medical condition, suggest a qualified coach or clinician for that part, and keep your advice general.

## 13. Move-on bar vs mastery bar

The failed course gated every lesson on a mastery criterion that took weeks ("name any note on any string in about a second, twenty in a row"). The learner either stalled or clicked "mark done" without passing, and the review schedule then ran on false data.

- **Move-on bar:** good enough to continue. Reachable in a few sessions. "Name 20 random frets on the low E and A strings in under 40 seconds, two separate days."
- **Mastery bar:** the long-term goal, revisited in later review. "Any string, any fret, about a second each."
- **A time limit:** "If you haven't passed the move-on bar in two weeks, move on anyway and keep this in your warm-up."
- **Long habits are not gates.** "Record yourself monthly for a year" is a habit to keep, not a lesson to pass.
- **Run parallel tracks** for a longer course (for guitar: technique, fretboard knowledge, ear, songs), so being stuck on one doesn't block the others.

## 14. A session plan

For a 20 to 30 minute practice session (v0; the shape follows common teaching practice. JustinGuitar's official pages, seen only as search snippets, describe about 20-minute sessions with a minute per chord-change pair, which fits but isn't verified):

> **Warm-up (3 min):** something easy from earlier work, to get hands moving.
> **Review (5 min):** one earlier skill, tested against its criterion. Record pass or miss.
> **New skill (8 to 10 min):** the smallest unit, tempo ladder, report count.
> **Music (8 to 10 min):** a song or piece using only skills learned so far.
> **Close (1 min):** note today's numbers (tempo reached, clean count) for next time.

Always end by playing music, or doing the real thing.

If the learner's time is short, split into two shorter sessions in the day. A plan should fit the time they told you they have.

## 15. Verify every tab, chord and count

The failed guitar course had about 30 wrong tabs and note labels, written from memory. Compute every one (see `topic-research.md` section 10 for a fretboard function):

- Every fret-to-note mapping, from the string and fret.
- Every chord spelling (which notes, which degrees) against its name.
- Every scale shape: is every note in the scale? any duplicates at string crossings?
- Every interval claimed (same fret on the B and high E strings is a fourth, not a third).
- Rhythm arithmetic: how many beats does the figure take? Where does it end?
- Fingering advice: consistent across lessons.
- Tab orientation: one convention, stated once, used everywhere.

Generate tab, diagrams and answer keys from the same function so they can't disagree.

## 16. What not to build

Stay within what a chat and a static page can do:

- **Don't build:** microphone pitch detection, audio analysis of the learner's playing, video or pose analysis, anything with a server.
- **Do build, when useful:** a metronome, a page that plays reference tones or chords, a tempo-ladder tracker that stores the learner's entered results, a timed note-naming quiz on a clickable fretboard (the page checks clicks against computed answers), a record-and-compare checklist, a report-back box that pre-fills a message for Claude.

A widget only earns a place if a step in the lesson tells the learner to use it and asks them something with it.

## 17. Beyond music

The same design works for any physical skill:

- **Sport:** observable counts (makes out of ten), slow-motion video with a checklist, the smallest unit (the toss, the footwork), a ladder of difficulty, safety and load.
- **Drawing:** a reference photo to compare against, measurable checks (proportions with a pencil held at arm's length, a ruler for perspective lines), timed studies.
- **Typing:** accuracy before speed, words-per-minute ladders at a fixed accuracy floor, posture checks.
- **Handwriting or calligraphy:** a model letter to compare against, a photo of their attempt, a few observable criteria (consistent slant, baseline).
- **Cooking technique:** observable outcomes (even dice size, a measured thickness), a photo against a reference.

## 18. Mistakes to avoid

- Praising technique you can't observe.
- A body-part instruction ("elbow in") as the only cue.
- "Practice 10,000 hours" or minutes-based plans with no criterion.
- Commenting on every attempt.
- Presenting external focus or blocked-versus-mixed as settled.
- A page that pretends to detect pitch, rhythm or posture.

## 19. Mini-transcripts

### 19.1 Bad

> **Learner:** I can play G, C, D, Em and Am. How do I get better?
>
> **Claude:** Great start! To improve, learn the notes on the fretboard, then intervals, then the major scale, then triads in all inversions… [a 12-week theory plan] Practice every day and you'll get there!

*What went wrong:* ignores what they play, no song, months of prerequisites, no criterion, nothing to report back, and cheerleading in place of a next step.

### 19.2 Good

> **Learner:** I can play G, C, D, Em and Am. How do I get better?
>
> **Claude:** Those five chords cover hundreds of songs, so let's use them. Two questions: is there a song you'd love to play, and which change between those chords is slowest for you right now?
>
> **Learner:** I want to play Knockin' on Heaven's Door. Changing from C to D is slow.
>
> **Claude:** That song uses G, D, Am and C, so you have everything. This week's goal: the verse, with the changes on time.
>
> Today, 10 minutes on the C to D change only. Set a metronome to 50. Strum C on beat 1, then change and strum D on the next beat 1. Count how many changes out of 10 land on the click with every string ringing.
>
> Watch one thing: when you leave C, which finger has the farthest to travel? Tell me your count and anything that buzzes.

*Why:* starts from their chords, uses a real song from day one, smallest unit that fails, a tempo, an observable count, one thing to attend to, and a report-back. (The song's chords were checked: the common arrangement uses G, D, Am, C.)

## 20. Evidence notes

- Honor-system self-check design (observable criteria, record and compare, calibration, not-yet path, report back): Practitioner, built on the feedback evidence (area B) and the fretboard teardown.
- Feedback on physical skill without observation: no direct study found (notes F, J). Counts with an outside reference: Practitioner. Learner's judgment first: Practitioner; feedback-frequency research is Contested.
- Self-video with a checklist: Moderate (Mödinger, Woll and Wagner 2021; Ste-Marie et al. 2013, one child sample; abstract-level).
- External focus of attention: Contested (Chua et al. 2021 vs McKay et al. 2024).
- Blocked then mixed practice: Contested (weak in applied studies, 2024 meta-analysis).
- Practice strategy over time: Moderate, small (Duke et al. 2009).
- Countable exit criterion per stage: Practitioner (JustinGuitar, snippets only; note I pass 2).
- Submit-and-review loop: Practitioner (vendor sources).
- Slow practice and chunking: Practitioner to Moderate, survey and interview studies; low confidence (note F).
- Deliberate practice: Contested (Macnamara et al. 2014: 21% of variance in music, verified; a separate music-only meta-analysis, Platz et al. 2014, reports r = 0.61 after correcting for unreliability).
