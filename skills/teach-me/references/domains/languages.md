# Teaching a foreign language

This file applies the general retrieval, spacing and feedback findings, plus a first pass of second-language research (note J): input and output, corrective feedback, spaced vocabulary, and pronunciation. Those language findings were read at abstract level, and much of the classroom data is from children and adolescents, so use the direction of each finding and its limits, not its effect size. What's still v0 is marked. Don't present a v0 item to a learner as research-backed.

## 1. What carries over, and what's v0

**Carries over, with evidence:**

- Retrieval practice and spacing are among the best-supported learning techniques across materials (Dunlosky et al. 2013). Vocabulary and grammar are good fits.
- For learning **word lists**, blocking beat interleaving in a meta-analysis (Brunmair and Richter 2019, g = −0.39). So teach a new set of words together, and mix sets later in review.
- Feedback helps when it carries information, and praise without information helps least (area B).
- Learning styles don't hold up. Choose the format by the content: audio for pronunciation, text for spelling, both for a new word.

**From second-language research:**

- **Spacing works for language too.** A meta-analysis of L2 spacing studies found a medium-to-large effect; short gaps match long ones on an immediate test and lose on a delayed one (Strong: Kim and Webb 2022).
- **Input they mostly understand is necessary, not sufficient.** The claim that comprehensible input alone is enough (Krashen) is Contested and widely rejected; the weaker claim, that lots of level-matched input helps, is Moderate (extensive-reading meta-analysis, Jeon and Day 2016). Focused instruction produced large gains, with explicit types beating implicit (Moderate: Norris and Ortega 2000). So pair input with production, and give an explicit rule when a contrast keeps confusing them. Popular "never study grammar, never speak early" advice goes beyond the evidence; don't repeat it.
- **Interaction with feedback helps** (Moderate: interaction meta-analyses, which include corrective feedback). Whether producing output alone makes learners notice their gaps is Contested.
- **Prompting the learner to self-correct tends to beat recasting** in classroom studies (Moderate; section 6).

**Still v0:** session shape, frequency-based word lists, reading-level thresholds.

## 2. Diagnose with a production task

Self-reported levels ("I'm intermediate") vary wildly. Ask for a short production at their level:

- "Write three sentences about what you did last weekend. Don't look anything up."
- "Translate: 'I would have gone if I had known.'"
- "Tell me about your job in two or three sentences."

From their attempt you can see vocabulary range, which tenses they control, and their most frequent error type. Also ask their goal: travel, reading, conversation with family, an exam. It decides what to teach first.

## 3. Vocabulary

- **Teach words in small related sets** (food at a restaurant, verbs of motion), blocked at first.
- **Introduce each word in a sentence,** not as an isolated pair.
- **Retrieve quickly:** a few minutes later in the session, ask them to produce the words from a cue ("How do you ask for the bill?").
- **Mix sets in later review,** once each set is familiar.
- **Production, not only recognition:** "What's the word for X?" builds more than "What does Y mean?" for words they need to say.
- **Give them a return plan:** a short list to self-test in a day or two and again in a week, with misses coming back to you (see `retention.md` section 7).
- **For flashcards,** one word or phrase per card, with an example sentence, tested in both directions.

## 4. Grammar

Treat a grammar point like a procedure or a concept, depending on what it is:

- **A pattern** (conjugating regular -ar verbs): a worked example on one verb, then a near-copy, then fading. Subgoal labels help ("find the stem, choose the ending for the person, check for irregulars").
- **A distinction** (ser vs estar, por vs para, perfective vs imperfective aspect): contrast pairs and sorting tasks. "For each sentence, which one, and why?" This is the case where mixing helps: the learner must choose.
- **Explain the meaning before the form** where the distinction is about meaning. "Estar is for states and locations; ser for identity and characteristics" with examples, then the tricky cases.
- **Verify every example sentence** against a reliable reference if you can search, especially idioms and regional usage.

## 5. Production over recognition

Reading a correct sentence feels like knowing it. Producing one shows whether they do. Each turn should ask them to produce something: a sentence, a reply, a translation, a fill-in. Recognition tasks (multiple choice) are useful early in a set, and production tasks should follow quickly.

Give the production a reason to exist: ask a question they'd actually answer, plan something, disagree with you. When their message is understandable, respond to what they said first and then pick one form to work on; that keeps the exchange an interaction, which is the setting the evidence covers.

## 6. Correcting errors

In classroom studies, feedback that pushes the learner to produce the fix (a clarification request, repeating the error back, a clue about the rule, "try that again") beat recasts (quietly reformulating it), especially on free-production tests (Moderate: Lyster and Saito 2010, 15 studies). Corrective feedback overall has a lasting medium effect (Li 2010). The limits: mostly classroom data with children and adolescents, and results depend on the test. A recast in text is also different from a spoken one: the correct form is on screen, but the learner may never notice it was a correction.

- **Prioritize.** Correct the errors that block meaning, and the one error type they make most often. Don't mark every slip.
- **Prompt first when they can probably fix it:** "Check the verb in your second sentence. Is this one finished trip or a habit?"
- **Give the explicit form if the prompt doesn't get there:** "You wrote *yo sabo*. *Saber* is irregular in the yo form: *yo sé*. Try the sentence again."
- **In free conversation, a recast is fine if you flag it,** so it gets noticed: "(small fix: *fui*, not *iba*)". A silent recast may read as agreement.
- **Don't prompt every error.** Turning each sentence into a correction exercise stalls the conversation; one error type per turn.

## 7. Pronunciation: you can't hear them

You can't hear the learner, and you shouldn't pretend to. Pronunciation is an honor-system domain (see `motor-and-music.md` for the design principles). Aim for being understood, not for a native accent: a strong accent can be fully intelligible, and teaching can improve intelligibility without changing accent (Moderate: Munro and Derwing). Pronunciation software that analyzes audio shows a medium effect, but that's software listening to speech, which a text chat or static page can't do.

- **Pick the sounds that change meaning.** Minimal pairs (*pero*/*perro*) where a slip produces a different word matter more than general polish.

- **Point to native audio:** a dictionary site with recordings, a video segment with a timestamp you've checked.
- **Record and compare:** "Record yourself saying these five words. Play the native audio, then yours. Which sounds differ?"
- **Give observable targets:** "Is your *r* in *pero* a single tap or a roll? Compare with *perro*."
- **Describe the mouth position** for difficult sounds, briefly, and check the description against a reliable source.
- **Report back:** "Tell me which words sounded different and how."
- If your environment can take an audio recording and analyze it, say clearly what you can and can't judge from it.
- **Never say "your pronunciation was good"** about something you didn't hear.

## 8. Reading and listening

- **Level-appropriate texts:** most words known, a few new. If they're looking up every third word, it's too hard. Then pair the reading with something they produce from it.
- **Predict before reading:** "From the title, what do you think this is about?"
- **Comprehension checks that require production:** "In two sentences, in the target language, what happened?" not "Did you understand?"
- **Point to a specific listening segment** with a question to answer: "Listen to 1:20 to 2:00. Where is she going, and why?"

## 9. Checking

Written language is checkable. Check it:

- Correct their sentences yourself, specifically.
- Accept valid alternatives (there's often more than one correct translation). Say when theirs is correct but unusual.
- In an artifact, short typed answers can be checked against a list of accepted forms, with accent-insensitive matching as an option early on; free sentences need a "paste into the chat" box.
- Be careful with your own target-language output: check conjugations, genders and idioms, and note regional variation.

## 10. A session shape

v0:

> 1. **Retrieve (3 min):** words or a structure from last time, produced from cues.
> 2. **New (8 min):** one small set of words or one grammar point, with examples, then production.
> 3. **Use (8 min):** a short conversation or writing task that needs the new material and the old.
> 4. **Close (2 min):** they produce the new items from memory; you give the return-plan list.

## 11. Mistakes to avoid

- Telling them listening or reading alone will get them there.
- Silent recasts in text.
- Correcting every error.
- Judging pronunciation from their description of it.
- A static vocabulary list with no plan to come back to it. Retrieve new words later in the session, then give a return plan; in an artifact, a review queue built from their misses.
- Presenting "prompts beat recasts" as universal; it's classroom data, mostly with young learners.

## 12. Mini-transcript

> **Learner:** I keep mixing up ser and estar
>
> **Claude:** That's one of the most common sticking points. Let's find where your line is. For each, which verb, and one word on why:
> 1. She ___ a doctor.
> 2. The coffee ___ cold.
> 3. We ___ in Madrid.
> 4. He ___ tall.
>
> **Learner:** es, está, somos, es
>
> **Claude:** Three of four. Number 3 is the interesting one: location takes *estar*, so *estamos en Madrid*, even though living somewhere can feel permanent. What do you think the rule is that makes 3 different from 4?

*Why:* a sorting task diagnoses where their boundary is, feedback names the exact miss and the rule, and the learner is asked to articulate the distinction rather than being given it. (The sentences and answers were checked: *es médica*, *está frío*, *estamos en Madrid*, *es alto*.)

## 13. Evidence notes

Second-language findings were read at abstract level (note J section 2); figures in secondary sources conflict, so none are quoted here.

- Retrieval and spacing: Strong (Dunlosky et al. 2013); L2 spacing: Strong (Kim and Webb 2022).
- Blocking beats interleaving for word lists: Moderate (Brunmair and Richter 2019).
- Feedback principles: Strong for direction (area B).
- Learning styles: Strong evidence against (Pashler et al. 2008).
- Input alone is sufficient: Contested. Level-matched input helps: Moderate (Jeon and Day 2016). Explicit instruction helps: Moderate (Norris and Ortega 2000).
- Interaction with feedback: Moderate. Output alone drives noticing: Contested.
- Prompts over recasts: Moderate, classroom, mostly young learners (Lyster and Saito 2010; Li 2010). Flagging recasts in text: Practitioner.
- Intelligibility over accent: Moderate (Munro and Derwing). Record-and-compare design: Practitioner.
- Production over recognition: Practitioner, consistent with retrieval evidence.
- Session shape, reading-level threshold: v0.
