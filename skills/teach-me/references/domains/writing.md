# Teaching writing

Helping someone become a better writer, as opposed to producing writing for them. The research for this skill didn't cover writing pedagogy directly, so most of this file is v0: it applies the general feedback, worked-example and retrieval findings to writing, plus common practice. Treat it as a starting point to test.

## 1. Where the general rules change

- **The temptation is to rewrite.** You can produce a cleaner paragraph in seconds, and that's exactly what doesn't teach. Rewriting their draft teaches them that you write better than they do. Point at what to change and why, and let them change it.
- **Quality is judged against criteria, not a key.** Writing is checkable against a rubric (claim stated? evidence for each point? paragraph does one job?), but not against one right answer. Make the criteria explicit.
- **The unit of practice is a revision.** The learner improves by revising their own text with a specific target, then seeing whether the revision met it.

## 2. First, route: do they want to improve, or want it written?

"Can you fix my cover letter?" usually wants the result. "Help me get better at cover letters" wants the skill. If unclear, ask: "Do you want me to edit this one, or help you get better at writing these yourself?" Both are legitimate. If it's for a graded assignment, see section 9.

## 3. Diagnose the draft

Read the whole draft before commenting. Then locate the main problem at the highest level it occurs, because sentence-polishing a paragraph that should be cut wastes everyone's time.

| Level | Questions | Common problem |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose and audience** | Who is this for? What should they think or do after reading? | No clear point, or the wrong reader in mind |
| **Argument or structure** | Is the main claim stated? Does each part support it? Is the order right? | Claim buried at the end; points that don't connect |
| **Paragraph** | Does each paragraph do one job? Does the first sentence say what it is? | Two ideas in one paragraph; topic sentences missing |
| **Sentence** | Is each sentence clear? Who's doing what? | Long tangled sentences; passive voice hiding the actor |
| **Word and mechanics** | Precise words? Grammar, spelling? | Vague words; repeated errors of one kind |

Ask the writer one question before giving feedback: "What's the one thing you want a reader to take away?" Their answer often shows the gap between what they meant and what they wrote.

## 4. Feedback on a draft

Apply the general feedback rules (`diagnosis-and-feedback.md` section 7):

- **One or two points, at the highest level that has a problem.** Not twenty margin comments.
- **What's working, specifically.** "Your opening example is concrete and makes the problem real." This tells them what to keep.
- **What to change, why, and a next step.** "Your main claim is in the last paragraph. A reader deciding whether to keep reading won't get there. Move it into the first two sentences and send me the new opening."
- **Quote their text** so the feedback is anchored.
- **Ask questions a reader would ask.** "What's the evidence for this?" "Who is 'they' here?" These point at the problem without fixing it.
- **Don't praise vaguely or soften into nothing.** "Good draft, maybe tighten it" is not feedback.

## 5. Model texts as worked examples

The writing equivalent of a worked example is an annotated model: a short piece of good writing of the same kind, with labels showing what each part does. (v0: this extends the worked-example evidence from math and programming; not tested for writing in the sources read.)

> Here's a strong opening paragraph for a grant proposal, on a different topic from yours:
>
> **[The problem, concretely]** "Every winter, about 40 families in our county lose heat because they can't afford a furnace repair."
> **[Why it matters now]** "Last year, two of them were hospitalized."
> **[What we'll do]** "We're asking for $25,000 to fund emergency repairs for 50 households."
>
> Which of these three moves does your opening make, and which is missing?

Use a model on a different topic, so they learn the moves without copying the content. Then have them apply the moves to their own text.

## 6. Revision as the practice

- **One target per revision.** "Revise only the topic sentences this round."
- **They revise; you check.** Compare the revision with the target and say whether it met it.
- **Before and after.** Ask them to say what changed and why it's better. This builds the judgment they need when you're not there.
- **Fade.** Early: you name the problem and the fix. Later: you name the problem, they choose the fix. Later still: "Read it as your reader. What's the weakest paragraph?" and they find it.

## 7. Rubrics and checking

Writing can be checked against criteria. Make them explicit and short (three to five), and have the learner check themselves first.

> Check your draft against these, and for each, quote the sentence that meets it (or say "missing"):
> 1. The main claim is stated in the first paragraph.
> 2. Each body paragraph opens with a sentence that says what it's about.
> 3. Every claim has evidence: a fact, an example, or a source.
> 4. The conclusion says what follows, not only what was said.

Then you check their marks. The gap between their self-check and yours is useful feedback: it shows where their judgment needs work. In an artifact, show the model answer and rubric after the learner submits, and have them mark each criterion with a quote from their own answer, or provide a "paste into the chat" box for Claude to grade.

## 8. Sentence-level skills

For grammar, punctuation and style, the general rules apply more directly: these are closer to procedures.

- Diagnose the pattern: one kind of error repeated (comma splices, subject-verb agreement) is worth teaching; a one-off slip is worth pointing at.
- Teach the rule with an example and a non-example, then have them find and fix the other instances in their own draft. "There are three more comma splices in the second paragraph. Find them."
- Retrieval: bring the rule back later on a fresh sentence.

## 9. Integrity

For anything that will be submitted for a grade: don't write text meant to be turned in. Do discuss their argument, react as a reader, point at problems, explain principles with model texts on other topics, and review their own revisions. "I won't write the essay, but I'd like to help. Want to talk through your argument first?" If you're unsure whether it's graded, ask. For self-learners and professionals improving their own writing, there's no integrity issue.

## 10. Mini-transcripts

### 10.1 Bad: rewriting

> **Learner:** can you help me make this paragraph better? I want to get better at writing.
>
> **Claude:** Here's an improved version: [a fully rewritten paragraph]. I made it more concise, strengthened the verbs and moved the main point to the start.

*What went wrong:* the learner asked to get better, and got a better paragraph instead. They learned that Claude writes well.

### 10.2 Good: one point, they revise

> **Learner:** can you help me make this paragraph better? I want to get better at writing.
>
> **Claude:** The detail in your second sentence ("the warehouse lost $2 million in a single quarter") is strong; it makes the problem real. The main thing holding the paragraph back is that your point (that the company should switch suppliers) only appears in the last sentence. A reader has to get through four sentences before knowing why they're reading.
>
> Try moving that point to the first sentence and adjusting the rest to support it. Send me the new version.

*Why:* specific praise anchored in their text, one point at the highest level that has a problem, the reason, and the revision is theirs.
