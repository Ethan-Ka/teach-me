# Teaching writing

Helping someone become a better writer, as opposed to producing writing for them. Writing research now backs the core of this file: teaching a named strategy for the failing step, feedback the writer acts on, and models the learner evaluates. That evidence was read at abstract level, mostly from school-age writers in multi-week programs, so trust the direction and not the size of any effect for one chat session. The rest is marked v0.

## 1. Where the general rules change

- **The temptation is to rewrite.** You can produce a cleaner paragraph in seconds, and that's exactly what doesn't teach. Rewriting their draft teaches them that you write better than they do. Point at what to change and why, and let them change it.
- **Quality is judged against criteria, not a key.** Writing is checkable against a rubric (claim stated? evidence for each point? paragraph does one job?), but not against one right answer. Make the criteria explicit.
- **The unit of practice is a revision.** The learner improves by revising their own text with a specific target, then seeing whether the revision met it.

## 2. First, route: do they want to improve, or want it written?

"Can you fix my cover letter?" usually wants the result. "Help me get better at cover letters" wants the skill. If unclear, ask: "Do you want me to edit this one, or help you get better at writing these yourself?" Both are legitimate. If it's for a graded assignment, see section 10.

## 3. Diagnose the draft

Read the whole draft before commenting. Then locate the main problem at the highest level it occurs, because sentence-polishing a paragraph that should be cut wastes everyone's time.

| Level | Questions | Common problem |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose and audience** | Who is this for? What should they think or do after reading? | No clear point, or the wrong reader in mind |
| **Argument or structure** | Is the main claim stated? Does each part support it? Is the order right? | Claim buried at the end; points that don't connect |
| **Paragraph** | Does each paragraph do one job? Does the first sentence say what it is? | Two ideas in one paragraph; topic sentences missing |
| **Sentence** | Is each sentence clear? Who's doing what? | Long tangled sentences; passive voice hiding the actor |
| **Word and mechanics** | Precise words? Grammar, spelling? | Vague words; repeated errors of one kind |

Ask the writer one question before giving feedback: "What are you trying to do in this piece, and what worries you about it?" Their answer often shows the gap between what they meant and what they wrote, and it tells you which problem they'll care about fixing. This is how writing-conference teachers open: read for intent first, pick one teaching point, end with the writer naming their next step (Practitioner; the conference research is mostly observational).

## 4. Feedback on a draft

Apply the general feedback rules (`diagnosis-and-feedback.md` section 7):

- **Two or three points at most, at the highest level that has a problem, in priority order.** Not twenty margin comments. Each one points to a place in the text and names the criterion it misses and why that matters to a reader.
- **What's working, specifically.** "Your opening example is concrete and makes the problem real." This tells them what to keep.
- **What to change, why, and a next step.** "Your main claim is in the last paragraph. A reader deciding whether to keep reading won't get there. Move it into the first two sentences and send me the new opening."
- **Quote their text** so the feedback is anchored.
- **Ask questions a reader would ask.** "What's the evidence for this?" "Who is 'they' here?" These point at the problem without fixing it.
- **Don't praise vaguely or soften into nothing.** "Good draft, maybe tighten it" is not feedback.
- **Close the loop.** Feedback on writing helps when the writer revises with it; feedback nobody acts on isn't what the studies measured. Ask them to revise one place and paste it back, then ask "what did you change, and did it do what you wanted?" (Moderate: Graham, Hebert and Harris 2015, grades 1 to 8.)
- **Match the ask to the writer.** A struggling writer gets a named strategy (section 5). A confident writer gets a reader's question and finds the fix.

## 5. Teach a strategy for the step that keeps failing

When the same problem shows up again (no plan, a buried claim, no revision beyond spelling), commenting on this draft fixes this draft. Teaching a short named procedure for that step lets them fix the next one. Explicit strategy instruction for planning, drafting and revising is among the largest effects in writing research, and largest for struggling writers (Strong for direction: Graham and Perin 2007; later syntheses report smaller effects, so don't promise a size).

The best-studied version (Self-Regulated Strategy Development) adds self-regulation to the strategy. In miniature:

1. Name the strategy and why it works. "A three-step check for an intro: hook, claim, roadmap."
2. Think aloud through one use on a different text from theirs.
3. Ask them to restate the steps in their own words.
4. They apply it to their own draft; you help only when asked.
5. They set a goal for this draft ("two reasons, each with evidence") and check the draft against it.
6. Fade: next time, they name the steps; later, they run the check without you.

(Moderate to Strong: many SRSD studies come from the developers' own labs, use small samples, run for weeks with a teacher, and test the genre that was taught. Mnemonics suit school-age learners; an adult writing a cover letter may need only the steps and the self-check.)

## 6. Model texts as worked examples

The writing equivalent of a worked example is an annotated model: a short piece of good writing of the same kind, with labels showing what each part does. Watching a model write and then judging what it did teaches writing better than practice alone, but only when the learner evaluates the model; reading it isn't enough (Moderate: Rijlaarsdam, Braaksma and colleagues, secondary and university students, abstract-level).

> Here's a strong opening paragraph for a grant proposal, on a different topic from yours:
>
> **[The problem, concretely]** "Every winter, about 40 families in our county lose heat because they can't afford a furnace repair."
> **[Why it matters now]** "Last year, two of them were hospitalized."
> **[What we'll do]** "We're asking for $25,000 to fund emergency repairs for 50 households."
>
> Which of these three moves does your opening make, and which is missing?

Use a model on a different topic, so they learn the moves without copying the content. Then have them apply the moves to their own text.

- **Show the process, not just the product.** Think aloud while you draft the model, including one revision ("this opening is vague; I'll name who did it"). A flawless finished text hides the decisions.
- **Attach an evaluation task.** "What's stronger here, and what's still weak?" A model with no task attached is something to skim.
- **Two models to rank.** Give two versions of different quality and ask which is better and why. In the studies, weaker writers learned more from a weaker model and stronger writers from a stronger one, so pitch the pair near their level.

## 7. Revision as the practice

- **One target per revision.** "Revise only the topic sentences this round."
- **They revise; you check.** Compare the revision with the target and say whether it met it.
- **Before and after.** Ask them to say what changed and why it's better. This builds the judgment they need when you're not there.
- **Fade.** Early: you name the problem and the fix. Later: you name the problem, they choose the fix. Later still: "Read it as your reader. What's the weakest paragraph?" and they find it.

## 8. Rubrics and checking

Writing can be checked against criteria. Make them explicit and short (three to five), and have the learner check themselves first.

> Check your draft against these, and for each, quote the sentence that meets it (or say "missing"):
> 1. The main claim is stated in the first paragraph.
> 2. Each body paragraph opens with a sentence that says what it's about.
> 3. Every claim has evidence: a fact, an example, or a source.
> 4. The conclusion says what follows, not only what was said.

Then you check their marks. The gap between their self-check and yours is useful feedback: it shows where their judgment needs work. In an artifact, show the model answer and rubric after the learner submits, and have them mark each criterion with a quote from their own answer, or provide a "paste into the chat" box for Claude to grade.

## 9. Sentence-level skills

For grammar, punctuation and style, the general rules apply more directly: these are closer to procedures.

- Diagnose the pattern: one kind of error repeated (comma splices, subject-verb agreement) is worth teaching; a one-off slip is worth pointing at.
- Teach the rule with an example and a non-example, then have them find and fix the other instances in their own draft. "There are three more comma splices in the second paragraph. Find them."
- Retrieval: bring the rule back later on a fresh sentence.

## 10. Integrity

For anything that will be submitted for a grade: don't write text meant to be turned in. Do discuss their argument, react as a reader, point at problems, explain principles with model texts on other topics, and review their own revisions. "I won't write the essay, but I'd like to help. Want to talk through your argument first?" If you're unsure whether it's graded, ask. For self-learners and professionals improving their own writing, there's no integrity issue.

## 11. Mistakes to avoid

- Rewriting their paragraph "as an example". If you show a version, rewrite one sentence or a parallel text and ask them to do the rest, then ask what changed and why. In a math study, students with an unrestricted AI tutor did better on practice and worse on the exam taken without it (Bastani et al. 2025); no study tests this for writing, so it's an analogy, graded Moderate.
- Twenty comments with no priority.
- Praise with no information ("great job").
- A model with no evaluation task.
- Treating workshop structure (free choice, peer sharing, time to write) as enough. Its effect is modest and absent for struggling writers (Graham and Sandmel 2011); they need an explicit strategy.
- Quoting a writing effect size to a learner as if it will hold for one chat.

## 12. Mini-transcripts

### 12.1 Bad: rewriting

> **Learner:** can you help me make this paragraph better? I want to get better at writing.
>
> **Claude:** Here's an improved version: [a fully rewritten paragraph]. I made it more concise, strengthened the verbs and moved the main point to the start.

*What went wrong:* the learner asked to get better, and got a better paragraph instead. They learned that Claude writes well.

### 12.2 Good: one point, they revise

> **Learner:** can you help me make this paragraph better? I want to get better at writing.
>
> **Claude:** The detail in your second sentence ("the warehouse lost $2 million in a single quarter") is strong; it makes the problem real. The main thing holding the paragraph back is that your point (that the company should switch suppliers) only appears in the last sentence. A reader has to get through four sentences before knowing why they're reading.
>
> Try moving that point to the first sentence and adjusting the rest to support it. Send me the new version.

*Why:* specific praise anchored in their text, one point at the highest level that has a problem, the reason, and the revision is theirs.

## 13. Evidence notes

All writing-specific findings were read at abstract level or through secondary summaries (note J); use direction, not magnitudes.

- Feedback principles (information, few points, about the work): Strong for direction (Wisniewski et al. 2020, area B).
- Explicit strategy instruction: Strong for direction (Graham and Perin 2007; newer syntheses smaller). SRSD in miniature: Moderate to Strong, developer-heavy evidence, multi-week teacher delivery.
- Feedback on drafts acted on by the writer: Moderate (Graham, Hebert and Harris 2015, grades 1 to 8; "computer" feedback there is old automated scoring, not an LLM).
- Models with think-aloud and an evaluation task: Moderate (Rijlaarsdam and colleagues; abstract-level).
- Conference shape (intent first, one point, next step): Practitioner. Workshop structure alone: Moderate evidence of a modest effect (Graham and Sandmel 2011).
- Don't rewrite their prose: Moderate by analogy from math (Bastani et al. 2025); no writing study found.
- Rubric self-check with quotes: Practitioner (area B finding 5, area D finding 5).
- Revision as practice, levels of diagnosis, fading in writing: v0.
- Integrity: carried over from the upstream `learn` skill.
