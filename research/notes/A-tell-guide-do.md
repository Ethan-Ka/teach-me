## Findings

### Decision table: knowledge type x learner state -> move
- **Claim:** The right move depends on what is being learned and how much the learner already has. Table below. It is a synthesis, not a single study's output; each cell rests on findings further down.
- **Evidence:** Moderate as a synthesis (cells draw on worked-example/expertise-reversal, productive failure meta-analysis, KLI). The cell-level mapping itself is Practitioner (my inference). KLI framing (knowledge types matched to learning processes: memory/fluency, induction/refinement, understanding/sense-making): search summary only, not opened.

| Knowledge type | Novice (no schema) | Partial (some schema, gaps) | Fluent | Misconception present |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fact / association** (a name, a syntax token, a date) | Tell, then retrieval practice soon after | Cue-and-retrieve; tell only what they miss | Spaced independent recall | Tell the correction plainly; one retrieval later |
| **Concept** (why X works, what a term means) | Short tell with a concrete example and a non-example, then ask for an own-words restatement | Prediction or productive-failure style problem first, then consolidate; or guided questioning | Independent: transfer problem, explain to a third party | Elicit their prediction, let it collide with the case, then explain why (do not just assert the right answer) |
| **Procedure** (steps, an algorithm, a git workflow) | Worked example(s) with the reasoning named, then a near-identical problem with some steps blank | Faded example (blank more steps each round), or guided practice with hints on request | Independent practice, varied problems; drop the worked examples | Show the failing step by running it; have them trace it; then re-try |
| **Principle / heuristic** (when to use which; design judgment) | Worked example plus the principle stated; contrast cases | Compare-and-contrast cases they must sort; productive failure is viable here | Novel problem, then debrief | Contrast case that breaks their rule |
| **Motor / perceptual skill** (guitar chord change, touch typing) | Demonstrate, then immediate short attempt; the doing is the learning | Coach on one thing at a time between attempts | Independent deliberate practice, spaced | Isolate the faulty movement; slow it down; re-attempt |

- **Boundary conditions:** (1) "Novice/partial/fluent" is per knowledge component, not per person: someone fluent in loops can be a novice in recursion. (2) Productive failure cells assume enough prior knowledge to make an attempt (see PF finding). (3) For motor skills this session found no direct evidence; row is Practitioner and Background only. (4) Far transfer from worked examples is weaker than near transfer (search summary of Atkinson/Renkl).
- **What Claude should do differently:** Before choosing a move, name to yourself the knowledge type and the learner's state for this one component, using the learner's last answer as evidence. Then pick the cell. Default for a procedure with a novice is a worked example plus a near-copy problem, not an open question. Default for a concept with a partial learner is to ask for a prediction first. Never run the same move for a whole session; re-pick every time the learner produces something.
- **Example:** Learner: "teach me git rebase", has used merge only. Before: Claude narrates a rebase end to end. After: Claude shows one small rebase with the reasoning for each command, then gives a second repo state where the last two commands are blank ("what do you run here, and why?").
- **Failure tags addressed:** demo-not-teach, no-practice, over-questioning, no-diagnosis.

### Contingent tutoring and the handoff rule
- **Claim:** Scaffolding has three defining features: contingency (support matched to the learner's current response), fading (support withdrawn as competence grows), and transfer of responsibility to the learner. The Wood-Bruner-Ross-style rule is: after a failure, give more help; after a success, give less.
- **Evidence:** Moderate. Opened only as an abstract-level summary: van de Pol, Volman & Beishuizen (2010), "Scaffolding in teacher-student interaction: a decade of research", Educational Psychology Review 22(3), 271-297 (search summary; confirms the three features and that it is a narrative review, only a few effectiveness studies existed, and measurement is the main weakness). The specific "more help after failure, less after success" rule is Background (Wood, Bruner & Ross 1976; Wood & Wood 1996), not opened. A flipeducation.ai page calling the 2010 review a meta-analysis of 73 studies was NOT confirmed and appears wrong.
- **Boundary conditions:** Effectiveness evidence for contingent scaffolding is thin and mostly classroom case studies. Contingency requires an accurate diagnosis; if the diagnosis is wrong, "contingent" help is just misdirected help.
- **What Claude should do differently:** Treat each learner turn as a signal and change support one step in response, not three. Support ladder, lowest to highest: (1) re-ask or ask them to say where they are stuck; (2) a pointer to the relevant part ("look at the loop condition"); (3) a narrower question; (4) a hint that names the concept; (5) a worked step with reasoning; (6) the full worked example. Handoff signals, toward MORE support: two consecutive wrong or empty answers; same error type repeated after a hint; answer is correct but with no reasoning when reasoning was asked for and the learner then fails the next variation; long silence-equivalents ("idk", "just tell me" after real attempts); learner restates the task rather than attempting it. Toward LESS support: correct on first try with a correct reason; learner skips a step you were about to hint; learner asks a question that is ahead of where you were; two quick correct answers in a row; learner corrects their own error before you respond. After a success, next item should either remove one support or add one variation, not both.
- **Example:** Before: learner gets step 2 wrong, Claude explains steps 1 to 5. After: Claude points at step 2 only ("check what the pivot is after the first swap"); learner fixes it; next problem has step 1 and 2 left to the learner.
- **Failure tags addressed:** no-diagnosis, demo-not-teach, over-questioning.

### Worked examples, then faded practice
- **Claim:** For novices, studying worked examples beats solving equivalent problems; examples should then be faded (steps removed progressively) rather than switched abruptly to full problems.
- **Evidence:** Strong for the worked-example effect (Background: Sweller et al.; confirmed in Sweller's own words in an interview, opened: "Worked examples followed by problems is always better" for novices, https://www.ollielovell.com/johnsweller6/). Moderate for fading: Salden et al. (2010, Instructional Science) lab study, search summary only: adaptive fading > fixed fading > problem solving on posttest and delayed posttest; also "fading out was superior to an abrupt switch" (search summary of CLT book chapter, not opened).
- **Boundary conditions:** Mostly well-structured domains (algebra, physics mechanics, geometry). Weaker for far transfer. Stops helping, then hurts, as expertise rises (next finding). Worked examples with no prompts to self-explain are studied superficially (Background, Renkl).
- **What Claude should do differently:** For a novice on a procedure, give 1 to 2 complete worked examples where each step carries its reason, then a "completion problem" with the last step blank, then two blanks, then a full problem. Fade from the end backward. Insert a one-line "why is this step here?" prompt on examples. Do not give five examples in a row; do not skip straight to "now you try" with no example.
- **Example:** SQL join: example 1 complete; example 2 has the ON clause blank; example 3 has FROM and ON blank; example 4 is the full query from a plain-English ask.
- **Failure tags addressed:** no-practice, demo-not-teach.

### Expertise reversal and guidance fading, in actionable form
- **Claim:** Guidance that helps novices becomes redundant, and can hurt, for learners who already have the schema; so guidance should be matched to current expertise and reduced as it grows.
- **Evidence:** Strong in the lab, narrower in the field. Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003), Educational Psychologist 38(1), 23-31 (corrected in note L): search summary only. Sweller interview (opened): "As expertise goes up, the advantage of worked examples go down"; for learners with more knowledge, starting with problems is better. Caveat from search summary: many reversal studies use subjective cognitive-load ratings.
- **Boundary conditions:** Evidence is mainly on structured tasks and short interventions. Expertise must be assessed per topic. An over-confident self-report of expertise is the main way to trigger a wrong "less help" decision.
- **What Claude should do differently:** Get a one-question expertise probe before the first example, such as "Before I show you, try this tiny problem or tell me what you would do first", and judge by the attempt, not the self-rating. If the learner solves the probe correctly, skip the worked example and go to a harder problem. If a learner who got worked examples starts finishing the steps before you reveal them, switch to independent problems. Do not re-explain what the learner just did correctly; confirm in a clause and move on.
- **Example:** Learner says "I know Python basics, teach me decorators". Before: Claude walks through functions as first-class objects, closures, then decorators. After: Claude asks them to predict what a given 5-line wrapper does; they predict correctly; Claude skips to `functools.wraps` and a task.
- **Failure tags addressed:** over-explaining, wall-of-text, no-diagnosis.

### Productive failure vs. direct instruction
- **Claim:** Letting learners attempt a problem before being taught, followed by consolidating instruction, beats instruction first on conceptual understanding and transfer on average, but only under specific conditions.
- **Evidence:** Moderate, Contested. Sinha & Kapur (2021), Review of Educational Research, doi 10.3102/00346543211019105. Via search summary and a press article (opened: https://phys.org/news/2021-09-productively-wiser.html): 53 studies, 166 comparisons, Hedges g = 0.36 favoring problem-solving-first (PS-I); 0.37 to 0.58 when the design followed productive-failure principles; larger after publication-bias correction (0.87, search summary only; treat with caution). Moderators: smaller benefit for primary-school learners; domain-general skills favored instruction-first. Sweller (interview, opened) says tests varying one factor at a time give different results from those reported; this is a stated position, not a published rebuttal I read. Kapur's 2016 reframing (productive/unproductive x success/failure) is a search summary only.
- **Boundary conditions:** Needs (per Kapur, press article): some prior knowledge so the problem is graspable intuitively; a problem that activates prior knowledge; and, crucially, consolidating instruction afterward that compares the learner's attempts to the canonical solution and shows why they fell short. Younger learners, tasks needing domain-general skills, and fully novel content favor instruction first. Outcomes measured are conceptual and transfer; procedural fluency gains are small or absent (Background; not verified). Most studies are short, maths/science, with classroom or lab control.
- **What Claude should do differently:** Use "attempt first" only for concepts and principles where the learner has adjacent knowledge and the problem is graspable ("how would you compare these two datasets' spread?" before teaching variance). Cap the attempt at one problem, say explicitly that failing is expected, and always follow with a consolidation that uses their own attempt: "your idea of X captured Y; what it misses is Z". Do not use it for a novice with no foothold, a procedure they must just execute, or when the learner is pressed for time. If the learner flounders with no usable ideas after one hint, switch to a worked example rather than extending the struggle.
- **Example:** Teaching standard deviation. Before: define the formula. After: "Here are two teams' scores. Invent a single number that says which team is more consistent." Take their attempt (often mean absolute difference), then show variance as the refined version and where theirs differs.
- **Failure tags addressed:** demo-not-teach, no-diagnosis. Risk tag: over-questioning if used on novices.

### Guidance debate (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006)
- **Claim:** Pure discovery with minimal guidance is ineffective for novices; the productive-failure literature does not contradict this because PF includes structured instruction after the attempt.
- **Evidence:** Moderate; the 2006 paper itself was not opened (Background). Kapur 2012 lists the standard objections to minimal guidance (search summary).
- **Boundary conditions:** The disagreement is real for how much pre-instruction struggle helps novices. Both sides agree guidance matters and that fully open discovery is poor.
- **What Claude should do differently:** Never leave a novice with an open task and no support or no consolidation. "Let them do it" means a bounded task with feedback, not "figure it out".
- **Failure tags addressed:** over-questioning, no-feedback-loop.

### Claim check: "dialogue without diagnosis produces more engagement and no more learning" (upstream `learn`)
- **Claim:** Verdict: REVISE (soften or cut the research attribution). I could not find a study that tests this statement. The nearest work: Bastani et al. (PNAS 2025) found an unrestricted GPT tutor raised practice scores but lowered later exam scores about 17% relative to no AI, while a guardrailed hint-based tutor mitigated the harm to roughly control level (secondary and abstract summaries; PNAS paper not opened). That is about answer-giving, not Socratic dialogue without diagnosis. A search for Socratic-dialogue studies found only preprints and pilots, some showing gains with Socratic design, some arguing LLM tutors lack learner modeling (search summary; none verified).
- **Evidence:** Practitioner for the principle (diagnosing first is coherent with contingency, expertise reversal), but the specific empirical attribution is unsupported. Grade the sentence as unverified.
- **Boundary conditions:** Diagnose-first is well supported in principle; "dialogue produces engagement but no learning" is not shown by any source I opened.
- **What Claude should do differently:** Keep the behavior (find out what the learner knows before teaching) but drop "research finds that". Replace with a reason: a probe lets you pick the right cell in the decision table and avoids teaching what they know or skipping what they lack. Keep the probe to one question, and let a learner's own task attempt serve as the probe when possible.
- **Example:** Candidate text: "Locate the learner first, because the right move depends on what they already have; a leading-question chain aimed at the wrong level wastes their turns."
- **Failure tags addressed:** no-diagnosis, over-questioning.

## Contradictions / open questions
- Productive failure effect size and its range of validity are contested; Sweller disputes. Need to read Sinha & Kapur 2021 directly and any published critique (not found this session).
- "Watching someone do a thing is the most passive way to learn" (upstream claim): consistent with ICAP's passive mode (see C), but worked-example research shows studying examples is effective for novices when paired with self-explanation and fading. Verdict leaning REVISE: demonstration is fine as the first step for novices, poor as the only step.
- "Worked examples on parallel problems as the cleanest way to teach procedure": SUPPORTED for novices on structured procedures (Strong), with the expertise-reversal limit.
- No evidence gathered on motor skills or on how well the tell/guide/do table holds for open-ended domains (writing, design).
- Whether an LLM can reliably judge "partial vs fluent" from one answer is untested.

## Candidate skill text
> Choose each move by asking two things about the one idea in front of you: what kind of knowledge is it (a fact, a concept, a procedure, a judgment call, a physical skill), and what can this learner already do with it? A learner with no foothold gets a worked example with the reasoning shown, then a near-copy where they finish part of it. A learner with some footing gets a prediction or a problem to attempt first, followed by your explanation built from what they produced. A learner who is fluent gets harder problems and less of you. Change one thing per turn: after a miss, add one level of help; after a clean success, remove one. Reason: help that matches the learner's level is what transfers responsibility; help that is too heavy wastes their effort and help that is too light stalls them.
