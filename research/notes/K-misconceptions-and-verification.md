## Part A. Evidence for the newest rules

The skill's rules under test (SKILL.md sections on misconceptions, "Pick cases that discriminate", and the artifact checklist):
- R1. Derive likely misconceptions systematically from the rules (drop, over-apply, misorder, mis-scope, boundary confusion), not from intuition.
- R2. Write items that discriminate: each misconception gives a different wrong answer from the right rule.
- R3. Every path through a lesson confronts each misconception (at least twice), and a simulated holder of each is run through the page before shipping.
- R4. Confront the misconception (predict, collide, explain) rather than asserting the right answer.

### Finding 1. Systematic procedural bugs exist and can be generated from the procedure (supports R1, with a stability caveat)
- **Claim:** In multi-digit subtraction, a large share of student errors are not random slips but consistent wrong procedures ("bugs"), and these can be enumerated by perturbing the correct procedure (deleting or mis-ordering a step and applying a repair at the impasse).
- **Evidence:** Moderate for the existence of systematic bugs in arithmetic; Moderate/Contested for stability and for generality beyond arithmetic. Brown & Burton (1978), Cognitive Science 2(2), 155-192, DOI 10.1207/s15516709cog0202_4: abstract-level only (search summary): procedural networks used to diagnose student behaviour and generate diagnostic tests. Brown & VanLehn (1980), Cognitive Science 4(4), 379-426, DOI 10.1207/s15516709cog0404_3: opened (PDF text). Their abstract: "all bugs of a procedural skill can be derived by a highly constrained form of problem solving acting on incomplete procedures", modelled by deletion operations that "model incomplete learning and forgetting"; the theory should generate observed bugs and fail to generate "star-bugs" (absurd procedures never observed); tested on "an extensive data base of bugs for multidigit subtraction collected with the aid of the diagnostic systems BUGGY and DEBUGGY". The paper itself discusses that bugs "can be highly unstable (see the discussion of bug migration)" and that critics added to block one star-bug can cause another to be generated via a different impasse. VanLehn (1982) chapter, DTIC ADA169361 (search summary only): about 76 bug kinds observed across thousands of students; bugs appear and disappear over short periods, often with no instruction in between ("bug migration"); a bug is "a notation for systematic errors and not an explanation". Payne & Squibb (1990), Cognitive Science 14(3), 445-481 (Crossref record, search summary): in algebra, mal-rule frequency is heavily skewed (many infrequent, few frequent) and mal-rules are "very unstable", used irregularly. I could not confirm the "1,325 students" figure sometimes attached to BUGGY; do not cite it.
- **Boundary conditions:** Evidence base is multi-digit subtraction (plus some algebra and addition). Domains where the correct procedure is a short, fully specified algorithm give the cleanest bug enumeration; conceptual domains (physics, probability) have no deletion-and-repair grammar to enumerate. Many generated bugs are rare, so a generated list is a superset of what any one cohort shows. Bugs are unstable within a learner, so "this learner has bug X" from one item is weak evidence; a single wrong answer is a hypothesis.
- **What Claude should do differently:** For procedural skills, enumerate candidate bugs by mutating each step of the correct procedure (skip it, apply it at the wrong place, swap order, apply the nearest familiar rule at an impasse) and use the list to build items and distractors. Treat a learner's diagnosis as provisional until two different items point the same way.
- **Example:** Subtraction 305 - 128: "smaller-from-larger" gives 227, "borrow across zero omitted" gives 187, correct 177. Three different answers means one item separates them.
- **Failure tags addressed:** items-the-misconception-also-answers-correctly; coverage-list-wrong.

### Finding 2. Misconception-based distractors diagnose and are chosen systematically (supports R2 for multiple-choice)
- **Claim:** Multiple-choice items whose wrong options encode common student ideas produce interpretable, non-random response patterns and can track conceptual change.
- **Evidence:** Moderate. Sadler (1998), J. Research in Science Teaching 35(3), 265-296: opened (PDF text). Abstract: items with "highly attractive multiple choice distractors" administered twice to 1250 8th to 12th-grade students (Project STAR astronomy); for such items "instruction appears to strengthen support for alternative conceptions before this preference eventually declines", read by the author as markers of progress rather than impediments; this kind of instrument "has a profoundly different psychometric profile from conventional tests". Treagust (1988), Int. J. Science Education 10(2), 159-169, DOI 10.1080/0950069880100204: search summary only; the origin of the diagnostic-test development procedure and, via later work, two-tier items (answer plus reason).
- **Boundary conditions:** Distractors must come from documented or derived misconceptions; otherwise they are filler. Sadler's data are from astronomy with a fixed concept inventory and a large sample; a one-off lesson page has no psychometric calibration. The finding that wrong-idea preference can rise after instruction means a mid-lesson wrong answer is not proof the lesson failed. Two-tier tests exist partly because a right answer can be a lucky guess or right for a wrong reason (Treagust's rationale, search summary); single-tier items can mask a misconception that happens to share the right answer, which is exactly R2's concern. I did not open critiques of two-tier formats (for example, reason-tier options written by the test author may not match how students think); do not claim more than that.
- **What Claude should do differently:** Make wrong options the named misconceptions, each with its own explanation. Where a wrong-idea answer might coincide with the right one, add a "why" tier (pick the reason) or ask for a short justification.
- **Failure tags addressed:** items-the-misconception-also-answers-correctly; weak-distractors.

### Finding 3. Teachers who know the common wrong answer produce larger gains on misconception-heavy items (supports R1 and R2 as a rationale)
- **Claim:** Knowing the content is enough where students have no strong misconception; where many do, only teachers who also knew the popular wrong answer had large student gains.
- **Evidence:** Moderate (large observational study, correlational). Sadler, Sonnert, Coyle, Cook-Smith & Miller (2013), American Educational Research Journal 50(5), 1020-1049, DOI 10.3102/0002831213477680. Search summary plus an EdWeek report; full text paywalled and not opened. Reported: 9,556 students of 181 middle school physical science teachers; teachers took the same items and picked the correct answer and the most popular wrong one; on items without a misconception, subject knowledge alone predicted gains; on items with a prevalent misconception, only teachers who knew both the science and the misconception had large gains. I did not obtain effect sizes.
- **Boundary conditions:** Observational (teachers were not randomised), middle school physical science, multiple-choice measures. It supports "knowing the likely wrong answers matters", not "building a lesson around them causes gains". The claim applies to items with a very popular wrong answer, which is why a derived list helps only where misconceptions are actually popular.
- **What Claude should do differently:** Spend list-building effort on topics with known popular wrong answers; for topics with none, plain clear exposition is not worse by this evidence.
- **Failure tags addressed:** generic-explanation-misses-the-real-error.

### Finding 4. Refutation texts beat plain exposition for misconceptions, with a smaller, contested effect (supports R4)
- **Claim:** Text that states a misconception, refutes it, and gives the correct explanation produces better learning than expository text on the same content.
- **Evidence:** Moderate, with real contest. Schroeder & Kucera (2022), Educational Psychology Review 34, 957-987, DOI 10.1007/s10648-021-09656-z: 44 independent comparisons (n = 3,869), g = 0.41 (search summary of the abstract; PDF behind a login redirect, not opened). Danielson et al. (2024/2025), Educational Psychologist 60(1), DOI 10.1080/00461520.2024.2365628 (search summary): pre-registered, 71 articles, 26 moderators, consistent significant advantage over non-refutation text; moderators did not enhance or diminish it; I did not obtain the pooled effect size. Both note recent skepticism (Zengilowski et al., 2021, not opened). Tippett (2010), Int. J. Science and Mathematics Education 8, DOI 10.1007/s10763-010-9203-x (search summary): review of two decades; refutation text more likely than traditional text to yield conceptual change; no developmental trend found. Guzzetti, Snyder, Glass & Gamas (1993), Reading Research Quarterly 28(2), 116-159, DOI 10.2307/747886 (search summary): early meta-analysis; refutational text and other conflict strategies reduced misconceptions. Already in the skill: Muller et al. (2008), physics, randomised, refutation helped (cited in artifact-blueprint.md; not re-checked here).
- **Boundary conditions:** Mostly short texts, science topics, college and secondary readers, measured soon after reading; the studies compare text types, not full lessons. "Refutation helps" means the explicit contrast, not that the learner's own commitment was needed (that is Finding 5). Delayed-test durability is not established by anything I opened.
- **What Claude should do differently:** In a lesson or explanation on a topic with a known wrong idea, name the wrong idea, say why it is tempting, say why it fails, then give the right account. Do not only tell the right account.
- **Failure tags addressed:** right-answer-asserted-misconception-left-intact.

### Finding 5. Cognitive conflict (predict, then collide) helps less reliably than its theory suggests (softens R4)
- **Claim:** Presenting anomalous data or a contradicting case does not reliably produce conceptual change; it often yields only weak or peripheral revisions unless the conflict is meaningful to the learner.
- **Evidence:** Moderate as a critical review. Limón (2001), Learning and Instruction 11(4), 357-380: opened (PDF text). Abstract: cognitive conflict via anomalous data was "one of the most common conceptual change instructional strategies"; she reviews "the controversial results obtained in the application of the cognitive conflict strategy in the classroom"; difficulties include making conflict meaningful ("motivational factors, epistemological beliefs, prior knowledge, values and attitudes, learning strategies and cognitive engagement, and reasoning strategies, as well as social factors"), theoretical and methodological gaps, and practical classroom problems. From the body: "Even if no weak or radical change is produced as it was predicted, presenting anomalous data may promote the first steps of the process of conceptual change"; and "Conceptual change is a gradual process and not an 'all-or-nothing' process. Therefore, in general, a dramatically radical change cannot be expected just after introducing anomalous data in a short instructional intervention."
- **Boundary conditions:** Her review is largely of classroom science studies from the 1980s-90s, not of self-paced digital lessons. Low prior knowledge and low engagement are the named failure conditions. It is a narrative review, not a meta-analysis.
- **What Claude should do differently:** Treat one collision as a start, not a cure: follow with explanation tied to the learner's own prediction, then revisit with a varied case later (the skill already says to re-ask with integers). Do not tell the learner the misconception is now gone after one prediction.
- **Failure tags addressed:** one-and-done-confrontation.

### Finding 6. Knowledge-in-pieces: a counterpoint to fixed, enumerable misconceptions (softens R1 and R3)
- **Claim:** Novices' ideas are often context-dependent fragments (for example "phenomenological primitives") that are used appropriately in some situations and inappropriately in others, not stable wrong theories that can be listed and replaced; instruction should refine and reorganise them.
- **Evidence:** Moderate (influential theory with detailed case studies; not a controlled-effect claim). diSessa (1993), Cognition and Instruction 10(2-3), 105-225 (search summary). Smith, diSessa & Roschelle (1993/1994), Journal of the Learning Sciences 3(2), 115-163, DOI 10.1207/s15327809jls0302_1 (search summary of the SRI and Taylor & Francis abstract: the misconceptions framing overstates the novice-expert gap; refinement and reorganisation rather than replacement; conceptions are both flawed and productive). The PDF host had a certificate error, so I did not open the paper.
- **Boundary conditions:** The strongest case is intuitive physics. In rule-based procedural domains (arithmetic, syntax, a rule-bound policy or fee calculation) systematic wrong rules are well documented (Finding 1), though even there bugs are unstable. The two views are less opposed for practice design than for theory: both say to test with cases where the wrong idea and the right idea diverge.
- **What Claude should do differently:** Present the derived list as a hypothesis space, not a diagnosis. Ask the learner for the reason behind an answer, and ask a second case in a different context before labelling the idea. When the learner's idea is partly right, say which part and where it stops applying, rather than "that is a misconception".
- **Failure tags addressed:** over-labelling-learner-ideas-as-wrong.

### Contradictions and open questions
- Sadler (1998) reports wrong-idea preference rising after initial instruction; diSessa-style views predict the same kind of non-monotone path. This conflicts with a naive rule of "wrong answer after teaching = lesson failed".
- Refutation text effects are positive across two meta-analyses, but the field reports recent skepticism and my sources are abstract-level; do not cite the effect size beyond "moderate, about 0.4".
- No source opened tests an automated or simulated misconception-holder as a lesson-QA technique. Brown and Burton's BUGGY did use executable bug models to diagnose and generate tests (abstract level), which is the nearest ancestor.
- Whether a derived list covers the bugs real learners hold is untested in this repo beyond the iteration 3 blind tests.

### Candidate skill text
> **Treat your list of likely wrong ideas as a hypothesis, not a diagnosis.** Wrong ideas are often context-dependent, and the same learner can use one rule on one item and a different rule on the next. So one wrong answer is a prompt to ask why and to try a second case in a different setting, not a label. Build items from the list anyway: where a wrong idea and the right rule give different answers, a single item tells them apart.
>
> **Confront, then follow through.** Naming the wrong idea and why it tempts people, then refuting it, teaches more than stating the right answer alone. A single collision rarely finishes the job, so come back to it with a varied case later.
