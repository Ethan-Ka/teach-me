# H. Myths to exclude

**Research question:** Keeps unsupported ideas out of the decisions in plan questions 1 to 3 (what to tell, how to check, how to build material).
**Sessions:** 2026-11-11 (one pass, shared budget with B and D).

Grades here describe the strength of the evidence **against** the myth, as in the template. "Opened" means I read the page or text in this session.

## Findings

### Learning styles (visual, auditory, kinesthetic learners)
- **Claim:** Matching teaching format to a learner's stated style does not improve learning outcomes.
- **Evidence:** Strong. Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer and Bjork (2008), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 9(3), 105-119 (abstract page opened). Valid support would need learners grouped by style, randomly assigned to different methods, all given the same test, and a crossover interaction (each group best with a different method). They found "very little evidence" of that pattern, several well-designed studies contradicted it, and concluded "there is no adequate evidence base to justify incorporating learning-styles assessments into general educational practice."
- **Boundary conditions:** People do hold preferences and differ in some specific abilities. The authors say untested versions remain and the finding does not rule out every form. Different content does call for different formats (a diagram for spatial structure, audio for pronunciation), but that is about the subject, not the learner's style.
- **What Claude should do differently:** Choose format by the content and the task (show a graph for a trend; have them play the chord for a chord), not by a style label. If a learner says "I'm a visual learner", use that as a preference to honor when cheap, say that format should follow the content, and do not build separate versions of every lesson.
- **Don't rule:** Don't ask for, infer, or design around a learner's "learning style".
- **Failure tags addressed:** wall-of-text (when "style" is used to justify one mode), artifact-is-reference-doc.

### The learning pyramid (retain 10% of what you read, 90% of what you do)
- **Claim:** The percentages have no research behind them.
- **Evidence:** Strong that the numbers are unsupported; the exact origin story is Moderate. An ASEE conference paper on the cone of learning (opened; the extracted text does not name the author, a search summary attributes it to Stice) reports: Dale's 1946 cone was a ranking of media by concreteness with no percentages; the percentages first appeared in a 1967 article by D. G. Treichler of Mobil Oil, "didn't cite any research", and were later conflated with Dale's cone. It quotes Will Thalheimer calling the percentages "bogus". Search summaries also cite Letrud (2012) on the NTL Institute version and say the NTL's original research is lost; I did not open those.
- **Boundary conditions:** There is real evidence that active, retrieval-based methods beat passive ones (see D and Dunlosky 2013), but the specific numbers and the ladder (read, hear, see, discuss, do, teach) are unsupported.
- **What Claude should do differently:** Never cite "people remember 10% of what they read" or similar. When recommending active methods, cite what was actually found (practice testing rated high utility).
- **Don't rule:** Don't quote retention percentages for reading, hearing, or doing.
- **Failure tags addressed:** artifact-is-reference-doc.

### Unqualified growth-mindset claims
- **Claim:** "Believe you can grow and you will achieve more" is not supported as a general rule; effects are weak on average, with possible benefit for some groups.
- **Evidence:** Moderate. Sisk, Burgoyne, Sun, Butler and Macnamara (2018), *Psychological Science* 29(4), 549-571 (abstract via Europe PMC, opened): two meta-analyses, k = 273 (N = 365,915) for the mindset and achievement relationship and k = 43 (N = 57,155) for interventions. "Overall effects were weak in both analyses." Results hint that students with low socioeconomic status or at academic risk "might benefit from mind-set interventions."
- **Boundary conditions:** I did not recover the effect sizes from the abstract, only "weak". Subgroup benefit is tentative. This does not show that telling learners effort matters is harmful; it shows that a mindset message is not a lever with large, general effects. Task-focused, strategy-focused feedback (area B) is a different and better-supported move.
- **What Claude should do differently:** Do not lecture "you just need a growth mindset". When a learner is discouraged, give a concrete next step and evidence of progress from their own work, and a specific strategy for the error they made. Avoid praising "being smart" or "working hard" in place of information about the task.
- **Don't rule:** Don't use mindset slogans as a substitute for feedback or for lowering the difficulty.
- **Failure tags addressed:** no-feedback-loop.

### Study techniques that feel productive: rereading, highlighting, summarizing, "keyword tricks"
- **Claim:** These popular techniques are rated low utility.
- **Evidence:** Strong. Dunlosky et al. (2013) (opened; see D). Highlighting "may actually hurt performance on higher-level tasks"; rereading is low utility mainly because other techniques do better; summarization helps only skilled summarizers.
- **Boundary conditions:** Low utility is relative and context-dependent; a learner skilled at summarizing can gain from it.
- **What Claude should do differently:** Do not default to "reread your notes" or "highlight key terms" as the study advice. Offer retrieval, spaced return, and why-questions.
- **Don't rule:** Don't recommend rereading or highlighting as the main plan.
- **Failure tags addressed:** illusion-of-fluency.

### "It felt clear, so I learned it" (fluency equals learning)
- **Claim:** Feeling of understanding is a poor guide to what was learned.
- **Evidence:** Moderate. R. Bjork via APS Observer (opened): performance during learning is "an unreliable" index of learning; the blocked-practice group predicted better results and did worse (see B and D).
- **Boundary conditions:** Self-judgments become more accurate when learners test themselves first (not verified here).
- **What Claude should do differently:** Do not treat "that makes sense" or smooth in-session performance as evidence; probe with a production task (area B).
- **Don't rule:** Don't take agreement as understanding.
- **Failure tags addressed:** no-diagnosis, illusion-of-fluency.

## Contradictions / open questions
- "Active learning beats passive" is supported (ICAP, area C; Dunlosky, area D) even though the pyramid numbers are fake. Skill text should say "active methods work better" without numbers.
- Learning styles and "preferences": honoring a preference may help motivation even without helping learning. Not studied here.
- Mindset interventions: the abstract suggests subgroups may benefit; the boundary is unverified.
- The pyramid origin account relies on one conference paper whose author line was not in the extracted text.

## Candidate skill text
> **Things not to say or build on.** Don't sort learners into visual, auditory, or kinesthetic types: well-designed tests found matching format to style does not improve learning, and format should follow the content (a diagram for structure, a hands-on task for a skill). Don't quote "you remember 10% of what you read and 90% of what you do"; those numbers trace to an uncited 1967 article and have no research behind them. Do say that active, retrieval-based practice works better than rereading, which is true and has real evidence. Don't offer mindset slogans in place of feedback: the average effect is weak. When a learner is discouraged, show them progress in their own work and give a specific next step.
